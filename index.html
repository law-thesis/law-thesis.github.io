Occupational Gender Bias
in Large Language Models

Compressed for Graduates, Not Necessarily for the Rest

Large language models appear broadly fair — most occupations yield near-parity name distributions. But beneath that surface, systematic gender bias persists, concentrated in manual, transport, and service jobs. Its magnitude is predicted not by how gender-segregated an occupation is in reality, but by how many of its workers lack a university degree.

Motivation

Why occupational gender bias in LLMs matters

According to SHRM (2025), more than half of organisations now use AI tools in recruiting — writing job descriptions, screening CVs, doing candidate searches. The EU AI Act classifies such systems as high-risk, subject to the strictest requirements.

This matters because AI systems can cause harm even when they don't make the final decision. Representational harm — where models reproduce or amplify stereotypes — can shape what information is presented and how candidates are portrayed, before any allocative decision is made.

And representational harm can become allocational harm. If a model associates "secretary" with women, a system using that model to source candidates may favour female-signalling names — effectively discriminating at scale.

The pipeline of harm
01
Training on biased corpora
Models learn gender–occupation associations from text that reflects real-world labour market segregation
02
Representational harm in outputs
Stereotypes surface in generated text — names, pronouns, descriptions — reinforcing who "belongs" in which role
03
Allocational harm in downstream tasks
Biased representations feed into hiring tools, CV screeners, and candidate searches — producing discriminatory outcomes
04
Regulatory pressure increases
EU AI Act classifies employment AI as high-risk — auditing and understanding model behaviour is now a legal and ethical imperative
This thesis addresses three gaps in prior work
1 — Where exactly does bias persist?
Prior work shows models can appear broadly balanced — but which occupations deviate, and is this consistent across model families?
2 — Is the pattern systematic?
Most audits use small, curated occupation lists. Using the full ACS taxonomy (565 occupations) tests whether deviations follow a structural pattern.
3 — Can intrinsic metrics predict behaviour?
The link between internal representations and generated outputs remains unsettled. SEFAT offers a methodologically aligned test.

Result interpretation

Two explanations for the status gradient

The regression results show that lower-status occupations produce larger deviations from parity — but why? The findings point to two complementary mechanisms, which are best understood as working together rather than competing.

Explanation 01 · Training data

The labour market was — and for some jobs, still is — highly segregated by class

Sociologist Paula England (2010) documents what she calls the "uneven and stalled" gender revolution. Middle-class women entered previously male-dominated managerial and professional jobs in large numbers from the 1970s onwards. Working-class women did not make comparable inroads into male blue-collar work.

The reason: working-class women could achieve upward mobility by entering higher-status female-typed occupations (nursing, teaching) rather than crossing gender lines into male blue-collar work — an option unavailable to middle-class women already at the top of the female occupational hierarchy.

If LLM training corpora reflect these labour market patterns, models will have learned stronger gender–occupation associations precisely for those low-status occupations that remain most segregated in reality.

Explanation 02 · Alignment blind spots

Bias mitigation may selectively target high-visibility, high-status occupations

Even when alignment successfully suppresses explicit bias for benchmark-tested occupations, the underlying associations may persist. Bai et al. (2025) demonstrate that value-aligned LLMs can pass explicit bias tests while still harbouring implicit stereotypical associations — what they call "explicitly unbiased" models that nonetheless form biased associations.

The parity-compression observed in this study may reflect surface-level suppression rather than genuine removal of gender–occupation associations. The measure captures where this suppression fails.

Critically, the occupations for which alignment succeeds may be determined by benchmark coverage. Blodgett et al. (2021) show that dominant fairness benchmarks suffer from limited, inconsistent occupation lists — the largest deviations in this study occur in broad manual categories that tend to be absent from standard benchmark lists.

The selective coverage problem
Software developer, lawyer, physician in benchmarks ✓
Roofer, logger, construction laborer often absent ✗
If alignment feedback focuses on the first group, bias in the second goes undetected and uncorrected. As Ferrara (2023) notes, bias mitigation reflects "product design and policy decisions" — and those decisions may systematically favour high-salience occupations.
Key takeaway

These two explanations are complementary, not competing. Training data encodes real-world segregation most strongly where labour markets have been most resistant to change. Alignment then reduces this bias unevenly — more effectively where benchmark coverage is richest, less effectively for the lower-status occupations that both the training data and the benchmarks underrepresent.

Implications for practice

What this means for AI evaluation and alignment

The findings point to concrete, actionable recommendations for how bias is evaluated and mitigated in practice — and for why social scientific knowledge should be part of AI development.

FOR EVALUATION
Expand occupational coverage beyond convenient subsets

Audits that focus on a limited set of high-salience, professionally coded occupations risk giving an overly optimistic picture of model behaviour — because deviations may be smallest exactly where evaluation attention is greatest. Results here show the largest deviations appear in manual and service categories that tend to receive less visibility in standard benchmarks and occupation lists.

→ Report results across occupational groupings including blue-collar, manual, and service work
FOR ALIGNMENT
Bias mitigation choices shouldn't exclude groups by occupation class

If alignment procedures are evaluated primarily against benchmark-included occupations, model developers receive feedback to reduce bias for those occupations while bias in underrepresented categories goes undetected. This is not a claim that benchmark selection caused the observed deviations — but uneven coverage can leave systematic patterns invisible.

→ Test alignment against pre/post comparison including lower-status occupation categories
FOR SOCIAL IMPACT
Manual and service jobs are consequential even if less visible

Gendered portrayals of work can function as representational harm: they may reproduce cultural expectations about who belongs in particular occupations and thereby contribute to normalising existing inequalities. Manual and service occupations comprise large segments of the labour market — stereotyped representations in these domains can be socially consequential even if they receive less public attention than tech or law.

→ Normative concern extends beyond high-status professions
FOR AI DEVELOPMENT
Social scientific knowledge should be part of the development pipeline

Understanding why certain occupations show more bias — and why that pattern reflects class-specific labour market dynamics, not just statistical noise — requires sociological framing. The risk of overly optimistic bias assessments is partly a risk of disciplinary blind spots: technical audits without social context may miss the most structurally important patterns.

→ Interdisciplinary audit design as a methodological priority
Ethical considerations
Binary gender operationalisation
By inferring gender from first names, the analysis implicitly reinforces a binary gender ontology. Non-binary and gender-fluid identities are systematically excluded or misclassified — a representational harm in itself. The thesis uses the term signalled gender to reflect that this is a perceived, not actual, attribute.
Parity as a normative choice
Treating 50/50 as the desired reference distribution is itself a normative position. Alternative positions exist — for instance, that models should mirror real-world demographic distributions. The analysis uses parity as the reference but does not adjudicate between these positions.
Research for the public good
Research on gender bias in AI systems serves the public good by identifying and reducing discriminatory outcomes that can reinforce gender inequality — a goal aligned with UNESCO (2021) and OECD (2024) frameworks on AI ethics. Identifying bias is the first step toward designing systems that treat all occupational groups fairly.
Finding 05 · Llama-3.1-8B case study · n = 92

SEFAT: going inside the model

Do gender stereotypes encoded in a model's internal representations predict what it generates? To test this, I introduce SEFAT — a contextual intrinsic bias metric adapted for decoder-only transformers — computed from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's hidden states. Llama was run locally on HPC infrastructure using PyTorch and the Hugging Face transformers library.

What is SEFAT? The Sentence Encoder Factual Association Test embeds each occupation name in neutral sentence templates ("This person is a {occupation}.") and measures cosine similarity between contextual hidden-state representations and male/female attribute vectors — separately at each transformer layer.

Unlike static embedding methods (WEAT/WEFAT), SEFAT captures how the model contextualises an occupation rather than its surface-form token similarity. Layer −3 (third from the output head) yields the strongest predictive signal across all layers tested, explaining 59% of variance in Llama's name generation behaviour.

WHAT THIS MEANS
Intrinsic and extrinsic bias align — with the right metric
Prior work often finds weak intrinsic–extrinsic correlation. This study shows alignment improves substantially when both metrics target the same construct. SEFAT and name-generation both measure gender–occupation association in a generative setting — and that alignment shows in the data.
OPEN QUESTION
Do compressed models still carry bias inside?
OpenAI, Mistral, and DeepSeek compress outputs toward parity. Do their internal representations still encode stereotypes? SEFAT applied to open-weight versions could reveal hidden bias even when outputs appear balanced — the "explicitly unbiased" problem (Bai et al., 2025).
About

Methods & context

This website presents results from my MSc thesis in Social Data Science at the University of Copenhagen, completed in December 2025 under the supervision of Stephanie Brandl and Andrew Christopher Herman.

Generative audit design. Each model was prompted with a standardised template — "Create exactly 10 realistic first names for a fictional [profession]." — repeated 50 times per occupation, yielding 500 names per profession. Gender was inferred from first names using gender-guesser and bias quantified as Total Variation Distance (TVD) from a 50/50 parity baseline, following the HELM framework (Liang et al., 2023).

Why 565 occupations? Most prior work uses curated subsets of 20–300 occupations, introducing sampling bias. This thesis benchmarks against the full U.S. ACS detailed occupation list (2022) with raw unmodified titles — making it possible to detect where "apparent parity" masks concentrated residual bias in less-studied occupational categories.

The status proxy. Occupational status was operationalised using ACS variables: the share of workers without a university degree (no-degree ratio) and log median earnings. In nested OLS regressions with HC3-robust standard errors, education emerged as the more robust predictor — earnings lost significance once education was held constant.

Llama & SEFAT. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct was run on HPC infrastructure using PyTorch and the Hugging Face transformers library. Embeddings were extracted from all 32 transformer layers; SEFAT scores were computed using offset span pooling on contextual hidden states across four neutral sentence templates.
